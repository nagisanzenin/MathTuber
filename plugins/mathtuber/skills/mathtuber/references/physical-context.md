# Give the mathematical subject a physical context

Use this when a release reflection finds that correct diagrams feel detached from the world. It is an editorial experiment, not a universally validated teaching sequence.

Begin with a specific object and event. Its silhouette, behavior and surroundings should help a viewer recognize it without a label. Keep only context that explains the event or supplies a stable reference. A river bank and moving current marks help distinguish a boat's heading from its path; unrelated decorative foliage does not explain that relation.

When introducing a diagram, preserve the object's position or explicitly show the new projection. Keep quantities and corresponding features identifiable. Do not let a more detailed silhouette change an exact boundary used for measurement. If a drawing is rescaled or a physical system is idealized, make that clear where it matters.

Prototype a brief plain and situated passage before expanding into five films. Inspect the object, trajectory and mathematical overlay at intended viewing size. Improve or reject the composition when the setting competes with the explanation. A new asset or extra render cost is not a quality gain by itself.

Consider returning to the physical scene with one changed condition and a held consequence. Choose sequence by the subject: concrete-first is optional. Keep enjoyment, calmness, conceptual learning and continuation as separate audience hypotheses. Frame samples and transcription cannot establish them.
