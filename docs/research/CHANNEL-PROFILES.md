# A channel profile for mathematical entertainment

Research synthesis and proposed experiments • 5 September 2026 • Research proposal, not an implemented or validated system

## Decision

Develop channel profiles, but make them editorial and audiovisual design systems rather than collections of colors and templates. A useful profile should specify what viewers come for, how they participate, how mathematical relationships become visible, and which recurring visual and auditory features identify the channel. Episode design must remain free to follow the mathematics.

The working hypothesis for IVisualizeThings is: **a surprising mathematical claim becomes understandable because the viewer sees the mechanism that makes it true.** Recognition should come partly from that experience, alongside distinctive craft. This is our proposed creative direction, not an experimentally established optimum.

Keep three questions separate: Is this recognizable? Is it appealing? Does it help someone understand? Neither polish nor familiarity establishes learning. Completion and subscription behavior are additional outcomes, not substitutes for these questions.

## Scope and evidence limits

This is a targeted synthesis supplementing MathTuber’s existing 41-source research library, not an exhaustive systematic review. Sources below include instructional experiments and reviews, adjacent branding and aesthetic research, creator process accounts, and design standards. Transfer from those settings to short mathematical entertainment is an inference that needs testing.

Access varies. Full institutional abstracts, accessible paper text and official creator guidance were consulted where available; some publishers exposed only indexed abstracts or excerpts. The source register identifies these limits. No new viewer study, analytics analysis, uninterrupted viewing of the batch, or subjective listening evaluation was performed for this research. Earlier frame-based observations remain production hypotheses rather than measured audience reactions.

## What the evidence changes

### 1. Recognition requires learned associations, not merely attractive colors

A 2026 benchmark across 1,162 brand assets found stronger average distinctiveness for shape-based assets than colors. This is observational branding evidence, not a randomized comparison of channel designs. It makes a palette-only identity a weak bet; it does not make color useless or establish that every channel needs a mascot. [S1]

**Proposal:** develop a small coordinated family of distinctive forms, object treatment, motion and sound. Give these repeated exposure. Test whether viewers associate an unfamiliar episode with the channel after seeing other examples. A cold viewer cannot be expected to name a brand they have never encountered.

### 2. Stable identity and creative variation can coexist

Product-design studies found that typicality and novelty jointly related to aesthetic preference. They do not supply an optimal novelty percentage or a video-retention formula. [S2] Processing-fluency theory links ease of processing with aesthetic pleasure, while later work emphasizes expectations and epistemic goals. Ease alone is therefore an incomplete aesthetic objective. [S3, S4]

**Proposal:** make the visual language easy to parse while letting the mathematical event be surprising. Reuse the material language and meaning of operations; invent the episode’s world and central transformation. A new topic should not simply refill the previous video’s diagram slots.

### 3. Motion should expose relationships

An instructional-animation experiment with 84 undergraduates examined dynamic cues designed around coordinated events in a piano mechanism. It supports investigating relational signaling, rather than assuming that making objects conspicuous is enough. Its setting is narrower than entertainment or mathematics. [S5]

**Proposal:** distinguish motion that changes mathematical state, motion that directs attention, and motion that expresses mood. For example, a pairing operation should visibly establish correspondence; a removed game piece should leave a legible changed state. Decorative movement should not compete with either.

Mathematical fidelity is a design constraint: squash-and-stretch must not falsify an area argument, probabilistic animation must not imply false frequencies, and character expressions must not suggest that random objects make decisions. These are logical safeguards, not effect-size claims.

### 4. Personality can help, but it is not a universal learning intervention

A 2024 meta-analysis of facial anthropomorphism covered 33 experiments from 13 articles and reported positive average effects on comprehension, retention and transfer. Effects varied across outcomes and conditions; the work does not establish that giving every mathematical object a face improves a Short. [S6]

**Proposal:** allow personality where it supports the episode’s premise—for example, a game opponent’s anticipation—but preserve readable mathematical objects. “Playful” should describe timing, choices and narration as well as facial features. Test characters against an equally polished version without characters.

### 5. Sound identity and explanatory sound have different jobs

A 2026 study tested 90 sonic logos with 1,860 participants. Melodic recognition and sung recall responded differently to structural complexity; their apparent negative relationship disappeared in adjusted models. Remembering a tune is not the same as associating it with a channel, and neither establishes learning. [S7]

**Proposal:** define a family of timbres and event sounds, with an optional brief motif. Reserve distinct event sounds for meaningful actions such as a legal move or completed correspondence. Compose or arrange music to the episode’s emotional development; use silence deliberately. Avoid requiring a musical logo before the substantive opening.

The existing library’s background-music reviews and experiments give mixed, context-dependent results. They do not justify either continuous music everywhere or a universal ban. Speech clarity and task difficulty should guide candidate mixes, followed by actual listening tests. [S12]

### 6. Creator practice supports a system of choices, not one rigid workflow

3Blue1Brown’s own advice emphasizes concrete examples before abstraction, useful visualization and topic selection; it warns against using animation tools mainly to move text and equations. This is experienced creator guidance, not controlled audience research. [S8]

Kurzgesagt describes iterative scripts, visual sketches, consistent illustration and narration, and individually composed soundtracks. Its process illustrates stable identity with bespoke execution; it does not establish which features cause popularity. [S9]

**Correction to the earlier proposal:** do not mandate that every visual be completed before narration. Iterate the explanation, key visual and script together. Prototype the hardest visual claim early, then resolve voice timing and animation together. Avoid copying another channel’s recognizable assets or pretending its production process guarantees its results.

## Proposed profile architecture

These layers are a design proposal, not research-validated software requirements.

| Layer | What belongs here | What can change |
|---|---|---|
| Channel promise | Intended audience, assumed knowledge, emotional tone, viewer role, standard of explanation | Deliberate editorial revision |
| Channel identity | Shape and material families, type roles, color conventions, narrator character, motion vocabulary, sonic palette | Controlled evolution informed by recognition tests |
| Series or format | Puzzle, visual proof, simulation, mathematical investigation; expected audience participation | Different formats within the channel |
| Episode | Topic, metaphor, composition, visual mechanism, narrative structure, score, pacing | Broad creative freedom |
| Delivery | Aspect ratio, captions, interface-safe placement, audio mix and platform constraints | Destination and viewing context |

Keep mathematical truth and legibility as hard constraints. Treat taste, atmosphere and most pacing choices as preferences. A profile should contain rationale, scope, exceptions and annotated examples—not only numeric tokens.

Useful profile contents:

- Audience and promise, including knowledge assumptions and what “understood” means.
- Reference frames and motion clips, plus anti-examples explaining specific failures.
- Visual and sonic vocabulary with semantic roles and permitted variations.
- Narration examples demonstrating curiosity, precision, emphasis and pauses.
- Format-specific guidance and examples of justified exceptions.
- Asset provenance, licenses, profile version and status: candidate, emerging or supported by audience evidence.
- Evidence links distinguishing research-backed principles from channel-specific hypotheses.

Carbon’s separation of functional and expressive motion is a useful design-system precedent, not evidence that interface timing values should govern video animation. [S10] Color should not be the sole carrier of information: use labels, position or shape as well. W3C’s guidance supports this accessibility baseline, not a claim that our videos are automatically WCAG compliant. [S11]

## Candidate direction for IVisualizeThings

My preferred starting hypothesis is a **playful mathematical workshop**: objects feel manipulable, the narrator investigates alongside the viewer, and the pivotal moment is a visible transformation that resolves the question.

| Dimension | Candidate rule | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Show a concrete situation with an unresolved consequence | A title promises surprise while the image says nothing |
| Composition | Make the active mathematical relationship visually dominant | A small diagram surrounded by unused space and headings |
| Object treatment | Consistent edges, depth and material cues, suited to manipulation | Gloss and shadows obscure mathematical structure |
| Motion | Preserve object identity while showing what changes and what stays invariant | Cuts or morphs hide the step being explained |
| Type | Introduce notation when it names something already meaningful | Symbols arrive before viewers know why they matter |
| Narrator | Curious, specific, willing to pause at an inference | Constant excitement or a stream of declarations |
| Audio | A coherent sound family with scene-specific density | The same loop treats setup, thought and payoff identically |
| Ending | Resolve the visual question and offer a small new case | A slogan announces understanding without demonstrating it |

This does not prescribe a dark background, a particular accent color, a mascot, fixed shot lengths, a fixed duration, or a recurring catchphrase. Research cannot select those for us without audience and taste judgments.

For Chomp, a candidate execution would make the board the main stage, preserve the consequences of each move, use tactile event sounds sparingly, and present the strategic argument through visible board states. The key difficulty is making the strategy argument valid and followable; chocolate texture alone will not solve it.

## How to test before building the system

### Stage 1: compare coherent art directions

Create the same short passage in three directions: tactile workshop, luminous geometric mechanisms, and playful visual investigation. Use our own assets and retain the same mathematical claim, narration and approximate duration. A 20–40 second prototype is a practical production choice, not a scientifically optimal length.

Begin with Chomp, then repeat the favored direction on a structurally different topic. A style that only works for chocolate is not yet a channel system. Test initial overall preference, then isolate important differences in follow-up comparisons; changing everything at once cannot identify which feature caused the response.

### Stage 2: collect distinct forms of evidence

| Question | Suitable probe | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Recognizable? | Equal exposure to channel examples, then attribution of a held-out topic with logos and channel text removed | Quality or learning |
| Appealing? | Randomized-order comparisons and specific questions about pleasantness, interest and discomfort | Actual continued viewing |
| Followable? | Ask viewers to explain the rule and predict the next state | Transfer to a new problem |
| Learned? | A new but related case, with prior knowledge recorded | Long-term retention unless measured later |
| Sounds right? | Actual listening on phones/headphones; compare dry voice and candidate sound designs | Success for silent viewers |
| Want more? | Offer a real choice of another episode; later inspect actual audience behavior | A causal platform effect without a suitable experiment |

A small formative pilot can expose confusing moments and reasons for preferences. It cannot support precise uplift claims. For quantitative comparisons, define the primary outcome, a meaningful difference and sample-size requirements before collecting data.

Avoid showing someone three explanations of the same problem and attributing their improved understanding to the third style. Use separate groups or counterbalanced matched tasks. Avoid evaluating only existing fans; intended newcomers matter. Platform comparisons also need caution because topic, audience distribution and recommendation exposure can differ.

Agent review can catch clipping, inconsistent objects, missing steps and profile deviations. It cannot certify human delight or substitute for listening and comprehension tests. Watch the full audiovisual prototypes; contact sheets and transcripts are insufficient for judging rhythm.

### Stage 3: encode what survives

Only after a direction works across topics should we formalize a reusable profile. Reuse object rigs, material definitions, typography and audio families to reduce production cost; retain episode-specific composition and reasoning. Track recognition, appeal, comprehension and production effort separately rather than averaging them into an invented quality score.

The immediate deliverable should be one convincing reference sequence and its annotated design rationale. Producing five more videos before resolving this direction would test too many things at once.

## Remaining uncertainty

There is no demonstrated single best aesthetic profile for mathematical Shorts. This review supports separating objectives, making explanations visually meaningful, and testing a coherent identity across topics. It cannot choose our final art direction, guarantee greater retention, or show that the latest batch improved learning. Those require prototypes and audience evidence.
