# A physical world with visible mathematics

Cycle eleven hypothesis: give each mathematical subject a recognizable physical setting, explicitly connect it to a simpler representation, and return to the setting with a changed condition. The artistic objective is a small, peaceful film that invites observation. The instructional objective is a relationship the viewer can explain beyond that setting. These are distinct outcomes.

## What the evidence supports

Goldstone and Son (2005) studied interactive simulations with 84 and 88 undergraduates. Learners explored competitive specialization for twenty minutes before a different simulation. Changing from concrete to idealized graphics produced better transfer quiz results than three alternative presentation conditions. In the second experiment, transfer solutions were faster than after either constant presentation; this does not establish a speed advantage over the reverse sequence. The manipulation was modest, and the authors caution against broad interpretation. These were interactive learning tasks, not short films or tests of cinematic realism. [Primary paper](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327809jls1401_4), [author full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/250458226_The_Transfer_of_Scientific_Principles_Using_Concrete_and_Idealized_Simulations).

Kokkonen, Lichtenberger and Schalk (2022) randomly assigned 70 upper-secondary physics students to concrete/intermediate/formal instruction or its reverse. The approximately 75-minute intervention and post-test concerned electromagnetic induction. Overall adjusted scores were nearly identical. Their equivalence test excluded differences as large as the specified half-standard-deviation threshold for the total score, but did not establish the absence of smaller effects; some subscale comparisons were inconclusive. This single-school study challenges a universal concrete-first rule. [Primary paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2021.101524), [full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353431748_Concreteness_fading_in_learning_secondary_school_physics_concepts).

Neither study establishes that scenery creates calmness, increases Shorts retention, reduces mathematics anxiety, or makes an animation beautiful. The following decisions are our editorial experiment, informed by those limits.

## Design contract

1. Choose a specific object and event before choosing equations. The object should be recognizable from its silhouette and behavior, not only from narration naming it.
2. Let the setting explain something: the river carries the boat; a camera enclosure makes the projected image possible. Remove unrelated ornaments.
3. Preserve correspondence when simplifying. Match positions, boundaries, colors and time. Explain when a drawing changes scale or when a component is an idealization.
4. Expose one useful relationship through an operation or measurement. Keep the mechanism readable when ornamental details are hidden.
5. Return to the physical subject with one changed condition. Hold its consequence long enough to inspect; do not append a detached slogan.
6. Treat concrete-to-abstract-to-concrete as an optional composition, not a mandatory teaching sequence. Use another order when the subject warrants it.

## Prototype before expanding

Compare a plain diagram and a situated version of the same brief passage. Inspect recognition, focal competition, exact geometric alignment and the transition into the explanation. A more elaborate render is not automatically a better film. Advance only when the setting does useful work and the mathematical relationship stays visible.

The review should say what is observable in sampled frames, what requires uninterrupted audiovisual review, and what remains an audience hypothesis. A future viewer study should separate recognition, explanation/transfer, aesthetic preference and willingness to continue. Do not infer any of these from a successful render or an upload count.
